\begin{abstract}
This manuscript studies conservative transfer of edge-normal flux degrees of
freedom between nonmatching polygonal meshes in the plane and on the unit
sphere.  The method follows the mimetic polygon reconstruction of Perot and
Chartrand \cite{PerotChartrand2021}: in each source cell, signed edge fluxes
determine a local Raviart--Thomas-like vector field with constant cell
divergence and a harmonic gradient component.  Target edge fluxes are then
obtained by clipping each target edge against source cells and integrating the
source reconstruction on the clipped subsegments.  The manuscript also analyzes
an alternative based on scalar Wachspress coordinates.  We show that a
componentwise vertex-based Wachspress interpolant is not a direct substitute for
the harmonic mimetic reconstruction because its divergence and edge-normal trace
are generically nonconstant on \(n\)-gons.  Generalized barycentric and virtual
element H(div) constructions remain plausible future research directions, but
they require a different vector-valued space and validation program rather than
a basis swap.  The study also introduces a unified higher-order polygonal
\(H(\mathrm{div})\)-style split-moment family with user-controlled edge moment
order, a local decomposition into polynomial-divergence, harmonic-gradient, and
divergence-free completion modes, exact hard enforcement of the zeroth edge
flux moment, and weighted higher-moment fitting.  Numerical evidence from
deterministic planar and spherical structured and unstructured tests verifies
conservation, direct edge transfer, and sparse projection equivalence to a
\(5\times 10^{-13}\) absolute tolerance.  On refined planar quad patches, the
high-order extension shows average edge-flux convergence rates of approximately
\(2.26/3.39/4.46\) for \(p=1,2,3\); on refined planar Voronoi patches, the
harmonic \(p=1\) baseline and split-moment \(p=2,3\) enrichments yield average
rates \(1.89/2.77/3.34\).  The same split-moment hierarchy extends to
structured cubed-sphere and deterministic spherical Voronoi-patch transfers,
where a degree-elevated basis captures the leading Piola rational correction
and yields zeroth-moment convergence rates of approximately
\(1.75/3.08/4.57\) (cubed-sphere) and \(2.05/2.52/3.62\) (Voronoi patches)
for \(p=1,2,3\), with machine-precision target-cell closure throughout.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Conservative interpolation between nonmatching polygon meshes is normally
formulated for cell averages.  In that setting, a source-target overlap mesh
defines weights that preserve the integral of a scalar conserved quantity
\cite{DukowiczKodis1987,Jones1999,UllrichTaylor2015}.  The problem considered
here is different: the data are signed normal flux integrals on source-cell
edges, and the desired output is the corresponding signed normal flux integral
on each directed target-cell edge.  This is an H(div)-type transfer problem.
It must preserve flux balance around every target cell, but it must also give
edge-wise degrees of freedom suitable for compatible finite volume or mixed
finite element discretizations.

The central design requirement is therefore local conservation on arbitrary
source-target intersections.  If \(O\) is a clipped subregion of a source cell
\(K\), the reconstructed source field must satisfy
\[
  \int_{\partial O}\uh\cdot\n\,ds
  =
  \int_O \nabla\cdot\uh\,dA
\]
with the right-hand side represented consistently from the source edge fluxes.
The lowest-order compatible case is especially attractive because
\(\nabla\cdot\uh=d_K\) is constant in each source cell.  The overlap invariant
then reduces to \(d_K |O|\), which is independent of the detailed shape of the
clipped polygon.  This constant-divergence structure is the mathematical reason
the harmonic mimetic reconstruction is a good fit for conservative edge-flux
transfer.

The contributions of this study are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a planar polygon transfer algorithm based on source-cell mimetic
        reconstruction and directed target-edge clipping;
  \item a spherical extension using source-cell gnomonic charts, great-circle
        edge clipping, and a contravariant Piola flux map;
  \item a sparse directed edge projection operator that reproduces direct
        source-to-target transfer;
  \item a unified higher-order polygonal \(H(\mathrm{div})\)-style split-moment
        family with exact hard conservation of the zeroth edge moment and
        refinement studies for \(p=1,2,3\);
  \item verification on rectangular, planar Voronoi, cubed-sphere, and
        spherical Voronoi nonmatching patches; and
  \item a comparison with Wachspress and related polygonal bases, including the
        proof obstruction that prevents a scalar vertex-based Wachspress basis
        from replacing the RT-like harmonic reconstruction in this setting.
\end{itemize}

\section{Literature Survey}

Mimetic discretizations preserve the structural identities of the continuous
operators at the discrete level.  Bochev and Hyman \cite{BochevHyman2006}
survey the mimetic design principles, while Mattiussi \cite{Mattiussi1997}
connects finite volume, finite element, and finite difference methods through
algebraic topology.  Finite element exterior calculus (FEEC) gives a closely related
framework in which discrete spaces form a de Rham complex
\cite{ArnoldFalkWinther2006}.  In this view, fluxes belong naturally to
H(div)-conforming spaces whose degrees of freedom are attached to codimension-one
entities.

The classical reference point for such spaces is the Raviart--Thomas (RT) family.
The lowest-order RT space on a simplex has the form
\(\mathbf{a}+b\x\), has constant divergence, and is determined by integrated
normal fluxes on edges or faces \cite{RaviartThomas1977,BrezziFortin1991,
BoffiBrezziFortin2013}.  Nedelec's compatible H(curl) elements
\cite{Nedelec1980} and the FEEC literature make the same geometric message:
the location and type of degree of freedom should match the differential
operator being approximated.  For the present transfer problem, edge-normal
fluxes are primary; vertex values are not.

General polygonal cells motivate several related but distinct approaches.
Mimetic finite difference and support-operator methods construct discrete inner
products and divergence/gradient adjointness directly on polygonal and
polyhedral meshes \cite{BrezziLipnikovSimoncini2005,LipnikovManziniShashkov2014}.
Virtual element methods (VEM) avoid explicit closed-form shape functions while
retaining polynomial consistency and stability on general polygons
\cite{BeiraoDaVeiga2013}.  Mixed and H(div)-conforming VEM variants are
particularly relevant because they generalize Brezzi-Douglas-Marini (BDM) and RT-like behavior to polygonal
meshes \cite{BrezziFalkMarini2014,BeiraoDaVeiga2016,BeiraoDaVeigaMixed2016}.
The Perot--Chartrand construction used here belongs to this compatible
polygonal family: it reconstructs a local vector field from edge fluxes using a
constant-divergence term and harmonic gradients \cite{PerotChartrand2021}.

Wachspress coordinates entered numerical analysis as rational finite element
basis functions for polygons \cite{Wachspress1975}.  They are part of the wider
family of generalized barycentric coordinates, along with mean-value, harmonic,
Sibson, and maximum-entropy coordinates
\cite{FloaterHormannKos2006,WarrenSchaeferHiraniDesbrun2007,Sibson1981,
HormannSukumar2008}.  Sukumar and Tabarraei
\cite{SukumarTabarraei2004} demonstrated their usefulness for conforming
polygonal finite elements, and Gillette, Rand, and Bajaj
\cite{GilletteRandBajaj2012} established interpolation estimates for several
generalized barycentric choices.  Rand, Gillette, and Bajaj
\cite{RandGilletteBajaj2013} also showed a practical conditioning distinction:
mean-value coordinate gradients remain better behaved than Wachspress gradients
as polygon angles approach \(\pi\).  These works support scalar H1
interpolation on polygonal cells; they do not by themselves produce an
edge-flux-conservative H(div) transfer.

There is, however, an important bridge from generalized barycentric coordinates
to compatible vector spaces.  Generalized Whitney-form constructions build
H(curl), H(div), and scalar families from barycentric coordinates on convex
polytopes and recover classical RT, BDM, and Nedelec behavior on simplices
\cite{GilletteRandBajaj2016}.  This is the most promising Wachspress-adjacent
future direction for the present problem.  It is not a component-wise
Wachspress replacement; it is a different vector-valued finite element space
with degrees of freedom on topological edges instead of vertices that involves
a completely new assembly and solver strategy.

Conservative remapping between nonmatching grids has a separate mature
literature.  Integral remapping for ALE methods goes back at least to
Dukowicz and Kodis \cite{DukowiczKodis1987}.  Jones \cite{Jones1999} described
first- and second-order conservative remapping weights for spherical climate
grids.  Ullrich and Taylor \cite{UllrichTaylor2015} and Ullrich, Devendran, and
Johansen \cite{UllrichDevendranJohansen2016} developed arbitrary-order
conservative and consistent linear maps, implemented in TempestRemap.  Those
methods are closest in spirit to the sparse projection
operator assembled here, but the present method transfers directed edge fluxes
rather than scalar cell averages.

The geometric ingredients in the verification studies are standard.  Voronoi
diagrams provide deterministic irregular n-sided convex cells
\cite{Aurenhammer1991}.  Repeated half-plane clipping is a specialization of
the Sutherland--Hodgman polygon clipping algorithm to convex cells
\cite{SutherlandHodgman1974}.

\section{Mathematical Method}

\subsection{Input and Output Data}

Let \(K\) be a source polygon with boundary edges \(f \subset \partial K\).
The discrete source field is the signed integrated normal flux
\begin{equation}
  U_f = \int_f \mathbf{u}\cdot\n_f\, ds,
  \label{eq:edge_flux}
\end{equation}
where \(\n_f\) is the outward unit normal of \(K\).  The output on a target edge
or an overlap boundary edge is another signed integral of the reconstructed
field.  Conservative interpolation is judged by exact agreement of integrated
fluxes on source-target intersections, not by pointwise vector agreement.

\subsection{Level-2 Harmonic Reconstruction}

In two dimensions the Perot-Chartrand reconstruction writes the cell field as
\begin{equation}
  \uh(\x) = \nabla\phi(\x) + \frac{d}{2}\x,
  \label{eq:pc_reconstruction}
\end{equation}
where \(d\) is constant in the cell and \(\phi\) is harmonic.  We use a level-2
truncated harmonic polynomial basis,
\begin{align}
  P_1 &= x, & Q_1 &= y,\\
  P_2 &= x^2-y^2, & Q_2 &= 2xy,
  \label{eq:basis}
\end{align}
so that
\begin{equation}
  \uh(\x)
    = \frac{d}{2}\x
      + a_1\nabla P_1 + b_1\nabla Q_1
      + a_2\nabla P_2 + b_2\nabla Q_2.
  \label{eq:level2}
\end{equation}
All coordinates are expressed in a centroid-relative local planar
frame.  This reduces low-order moment coupling and matches the assumptions used
in the paper's construction.

The constant divergence is fixed by the source fluxes:
\begin{equation}
  d = \frac{1}{|K|}\sum_{f\subset\partial K} U_f.
  \label{eq:divergence}
\end{equation}
For any scalar test function \(H\), Gauss' theorem gives
\begin{equation}
  \int_K \nabla H\cdot \mathbf{u}\,dA
  =
  \sum_{f\subset\partial K} U_f
  \left(\overline{H}_f-\overline{H}_K\right),
  \label{eq:moment}
\end{equation}
under the lowest-order assumption that the edge-normal data are constant per
edge.  The known divergence contribution \(\frac{d}{2}\x\) is subtracted before
solving the small harmonic system
\begin{equation}
  V\alpha = r,\qquad
  \alpha = (a_1,b_1,a_2,b_2)^T,
  \label{eq:local_system}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
  V_{ij} = \int_K \nabla H_i\cdot\nabla H_j\,dA
  \label{eq:gram}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
  r_i =
    \sum_{f\subset\partial K} U_f
      \left(\overline{H_i}_f-\overline{H_i}_K\right)
    - \frac{d}{2}\int_K \x\cdot\nabla H_i\,dA.
  \label{eq:rhs}
\end{equation}
Here \(H_i\in\{P_1,Q_1,P_2,Q_2\}\).  Since the current truncation has four
harmonic unknowns, the local solve cost is constant per cell.

\subsection{Reduction and Conservative Overlap Integrals}

Because \(\uh\) is a gradient-form field plus \(\frac{d}{2}\x\), a line
integral from \(A\) to \(B\) can be evaluated exactly:
\begin{equation}
  \int_A^B \uh\cdot d\x
  =
  \frac{d}{4}\left(|B|^2-|A|^2\right)
  + \sum_i a_i\left(P_i(B)-P_i(A)\right)
  + \sum_i b_i\left(Q_i(B)-Q_i(A)\right).
  \label{eq:line_integral}
\end{equation}
For an overlap polygon \(O=K_s\cap K_t\), the conservative property is tested by
integrating the outward-normal flux around the overlap boundary:
\begin{equation}
  \int_{\partial O} {\uh}_s\cdot\n\,ds = d_s |O|.
  \label{eq:overlap_conservation}
\end{equation}
This is the local divergence theorem.  It is the key invariant that a
conservative source-to-target interpolation must satisfy on every intersection
region.

\subsection{Edge-wise Source-to-Target Transfer}

The overlap diagnostic above is necessary but not sufficient for a usable edge
remap.  A target edge degree of freedom must be computed on the target edge
itself.  For a directed target edge \(e_t=[A,B]\) with target outward normal
\(\n_t\), the implemented transfer clips the edge against every source cell and
sums the source-cell contributions:
\begin{equation}
  U^t_{e_t}
  =
  \sum_{K_s:\, e_t\cap K_s\neq\emptyset}
  \int_{e_t\cap K_s} {\uh}_{K_s}\cdot\n_t\,ds .
  \label{eq:edge_transfer}
\end{equation}
The segment orientation is inherited from the target cell boundary, so the
normal in Eq.~\eqref{eq:edge_transfer} is the target-cell normal rather than the
source-cell normal.  This is the edge-wise source-to-target transfer that the
current nonmatching tests now validate.

The map is linear in the source edge fluxes.  For each source cell \(K_s\), the
local reconstruction can be written
\begin{equation}
  \beta_{K_s}=B_{K_s}U_{K_s},
  \qquad
  \beta_{K_s}=(d,a_1,b_1,a_2,b_2)^T,
  \label{eq:local_reconstruction_matrix}
\end{equation}
and each clipped target-edge segment defines a row functional
\(L_{\gamma,K_s}\).  The assembled sparse projection is therefore
\begin{equation}
  U_t = P U_s,\qquad
  P_{e_t,f_s} \mathrel{+}=
  L_{\gamma,K_s} B_{K_s}(:,f_s).
  \label{eq:sparse_projection}
\end{equation}
Rows of \(P\) are directed target-cell edges, and columns are directed
source-cell edges.  This directed convention is important because normal flux
orientation is cell-local; a shared undirected mesh edge needs a separate
orientation map before edge DOFs are collapsed.

\subsection{Spherical Gnomonic Backend}

The spherical backend is a geometry-correct extension of the planar algorithm
for unit-sphere great-circle polygons.  For each source cell \(K_s\), the method
constructs an orthonormal frame
\[
  \{\mathbf{e}_1,\mathbf{e}_2,\mathbf{c}\},
\]
where \(\mathbf{c}\) is the normalized cell-center direction and
\(\mathbf{e}_1,\mathbf{e}_2\) span the tangent plane.  A point
\(\mathbf{r}\in S^2\) in the visible hemisphere is projected to chart
coordinates by
\begin{equation}
  \xi(\mathbf{r}) =
  \left(
  \frac{\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{e}_1}{\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{c}},
  \frac{\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{e}_2}{\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{c}}
  \right).
  \label{eq:gnomonic}
\end{equation}
The inverse chart is
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{r}(\xi,\eta)
  =
  \frac{\mathbf{c}+\xi\mathbf{e}_1+\eta\mathbf{e}_2}
       {\|\mathbf{c}+\xi\mathbf{e}_1+\eta\mathbf{e}_2\|}.
  \label{eq:inverse_gnomonic}
\end{equation}
Gnomonic projection maps great circles to straight lines, so a directed target
great-circle edge becomes a straight segment in every source-cell chart where it
is visible.  The spherical transfer therefore clips that projected segment
against the projected source polygon and evaluates the same line functional used
in the planar algorithm.

Let \(J=\partial\mathbf{r}/\partial(\xi,\eta)\) and let
\(|J|=\|\partial_\xi\mathbf{r}\times\partial_\eta\mathbf{r}\|\).  The
contravariant Piola lift from chart vector \(\mathbf{w}\) to surface tangent
vector \(\mathbf{u}\) is
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{u} = \frac{J\mathbf{w}}{|J|}.
  \label{eq:piola_lift}
\end{equation}
The inverse map used by the manufactured-field tests is
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{w} =
  |J|\,(J^T J)^{-1}J^T\mathbf{u}.
  \label{eq:piola_pullback}
\end{equation}
Equations~\eqref{eq:piola_lift}--\eqref{eq:piola_pullback} preserve normal
fluxes across corresponding chart and surface curves.  They also imply the
surface-chart identity
\begin{equation}
  \int_{\Gamma_K} \nabla_\Gamma \psi \cdot \mathbf{u}\, dA
  =
  \int_{\widehat K} \nabla_{\xi}\widehat\psi \cdot \mathbf{w}\, d\xi,
  \qquad
  \widehat\psi = \psi \circ \mathbf{r},
  \label{eq:piola_moment_identity}
\end{equation}
so the flux moments and the divergence relation remain chart-based.  The
surface \(L^2\) inner product of two lifted chart fields
\(\mathbf{u}_i = J\mathbf{w}_i/|J|\) and \(\mathbf{u}_j = J\mathbf{w}_j/|J|\)
is, however,
\begin{equation}
  \int_{\Gamma_K} \mathbf{u}_i \cdot \mathbf{u}_j\, dA
  =
  \int_{\widehat K} \mathbf{w}_i^T
  \left(\frac{J^T J}{|J|}\right)
  \mathbf{w}_j\, d\xi.
  \label{eq:piola_hodge}
\end{equation}
Consequently, the spherical reconstruction keeps the right-hand side moments
from Eq.~\eqref{eq:rhs} in chart measure, but replaces the planar Gram matrix
with the Hodge/Piola-consistent tensor \(J^T J / |J|\).  A scalar area-scale
factor alone is not sufficient for this vector inner product because the metric
couples chart components through \(J^T J\).

The spherical direct transfer and sparse projection use the identical clipped
segment functional.  A target edge that lies exactly on a source edge can be
accepted by two adjacent source cells under naive all-pairs clipping; the method
resolves this deterministically by retaining the source cell on the
target-cell-interior side of the directed target edge.  This tie break is
essential for cubed-sphere \(N\to N\) identity tests and for reproducible sparse
operator assembly.


\subsection{Constrained Reconstruction Solve}
The reconstruction is computed as a constrained local least-squares problem:
edge fluxes are enforced as hard constraints, while the remaining degrees of
freedom are selected by the mimetic energy inner product.  This avoids the
failure mode of an unconstrained minimum-norm fit, which can preserve global
balance while losing the moment consistency needed for convergence.  The
resulting Karush--Kuhn--Tucker (KKT) system is
\begin{equation}
\begin{bmatrix}
V & C^T \\
C & 0
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
X \\
L
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
M \\
F
\end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}
Here, \(V\) is the harmonic-basis mass matrix, \(C\) is the edge-integral
constraint matrix, \(M\) is the Galerkin moment vector, \(F\) is the vector of
input edge fluxes, \(X\) contains the reconstruction coefficients, and \(L\) are
the Lagrange multipliers enforcing exact flux recovery.

For highly irregular polygons, high-degree modes can be poorly conditioned.  A
Tikhonov penalty can be applied to selected high-mode diagonal entries of \(V\)
without weakening the hard constraints \(CX=F\):
\begin{equation}
V_{ii} \leftarrow V_{ii} + 1.0 \times 10^2 |A_K| \quad \text{for } i \ge 4
\end{equation}
where \(|A_K|\) is the polygon area.  The penalty is not part of the
conservation proof; it is a conditioning safeguard for higher modes.

\subsection{Why the Raw Nonmatching Transfer Can Lose Target-Cell Closure}

The local reconstruction on each source cell is RT-like in the sense that its
divergence is constant:
\[
  \nabla\cdot {\uh}_s = d_s .
\]
Therefore, if the source edge fluxes close on \(K_s\), then the source-cell
reconstruction is exactly divergence-free whenever \(d_s=0\).  This does
\emph{not} imply that the \emph{raw transferred target edges} close on every
nonmatching target cell.  The distinction is essential.

Let \(K_t\) be one target cell and let \(\Gamma_s^\circ\) denote the union of
interior source-cell interfaces.  Summing the overlap balances over all source
cells that intersect \(K_t\) gives
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{e\subset \partial K_t} U^{\mathrm{raw}}_e
  =
  \sum_{K_s} d_s |K_t\cap K_s|
  -
  \sum_{\gamma \subset K_t \cap \Gamma_s^\circ}
  \int_\gamma \bigl[\!\bigl[ \uh \cdot \n_\gamma \bigr]\!\bigr] \, ds .
  \label{eq:target_balance_with_jumps}
\end{equation}
The first term is the desired target-cell divergence integral inherited from the
source reconstructions.  The second term is a jump contribution across interior
source interfaces.  It vanishes only when adjacent source-cell reconstructions
induce the same normal trace on their shared edge.

This explains the behavior seen in the divergence-free Voronoi figures.  Each
source reconstruction can satisfy \(d_s=0\) exactly, yet the target-cell edge
sum can still be nonzero if neighboring source cells agree only on the
\emph{integrated} source-edge flux and not on the full pointwise normal trace.
Conversely, the quad-to-quad harmonic-exact case is nearly exact because the
manufactured field lies in the reproduced space, so adjacent source-cell
reconstructions collapse to the same global field and the jump term in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:target_balance_with_jumps} disappears to roundoff.

The same mechanism can be monitored directly on the target skeleton.  For one
interior geometric target edge \(g\) shared by two target cells \(K_t^-\) and
\(K_t^+\), define the raw edge-jump defect
\begin{equation}
  J_g^{\mathrm{raw}}
  =
  U_{g,-}^{\mathrm{raw}} + U_{g,+}^{\mathrm{raw}},
  \label{eq:raw-edge-jump}
\end{equation}
where both edge fluxes are oriented with the outward normals of their
respective target cells.  A discrete \(H(\mathrm{div})\)-conforming target edge
field satisfies \(J_g=0\) on every interior edge because the two outward
normals are opposite.  In the present implementation, once coincident target
vertices are merged, the raw transfer already satisfies \(J_g^{\mathrm{raw}}=0\)
to roundoff because each shared target edge is integrated geometrically and then
seen from two opposite target-cell orientations.  This is an important
distinction: the nonzero target-cell divergence residual in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:target_balance_with_jumps} is therefore \emph{not} caused by a
two-sided target-edge skeleton.

The relevant raw compatibility defect is instead the target-cell balance
residual
\begin{equation}
  r_t^{\mathrm{raw}}
  =
  \sum_{e\subset\partial K_t} U_e^{\mathrm{raw}} - b_t .
  \label{eq:raw-target-balance-residual}
\end{equation}
The global constrained solve sets this residual to zero while preserving a
single-valued target-edge flux field.  Under smooth-field, shape-regular
refinement, adjacent source-cell reconstructions become more consistent
approximations to the same continuum normal trace, so the source-interface jump
term in Eq.~\eqref{eq:target_balance_with_jumps}, the balance residual
\(r_t^{\mathrm{raw}}\), and the size of the global correction all decrease.
The convergence is therefore toward the same continuum edge-normal flux and the
same continuum cell-balance constraints, not toward a spatially homogeneous
field.

\subsection{Global H(div)-Conforming Target-Skeleton Projection}

To remove the jump-induced target-cell closure defect without changing the local
source reconstruction, a second global stage is added on the \emph{target
skeleton}.  The unknowns are no longer directed target-cell edges; instead, one
unique flux \(x_g\) is introduced for each geometric target edge \(g\).  A
canonical orientation is chosen for each geometric edge, and every directed
target-cell edge \(e\) is related to the unique flux through a sign
\(\sigma_e\in\{-1,+1\}\).

Let \(u^{\mathrm{raw}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_d}\) be the vector of raw directed
target-edge fluxes, let \(x\in\mathbb{R}^{N_g}\) be the vector of unique target
edge fluxes, and let \(B\in\{-1,0,1\}^{N_d\times N_g}\) map unique edges to
directed edges.  Then
\[
  u^{\mathrm{conf}} = Bx .
\]
Let \(A\in\{-1,0,1\}^{N_t\times N_g}\) be the target cell-edge incidence
matrix, so that \(Ax\) is the vector of target-cell divergence integrals.  The
desired right-hand side is assembled directly from the source reconstructions:
\begin{equation}
  b_t = \sum_{K_s} d_s |K_t \cap K_s| .
  \label{eq:target_divergence_rhs}
\end{equation}
Here \(|K_t \cap K_s|\) is the overlap area in the same chart measure used by
the local source reconstruction.  For divergence-free source reconstructions,
\(b_t=0\) for every target cell.

The conforming target-edge field is defined by the constrained least-squares
problem
\begin{equation}
  \min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N_g}} \frac{1}{2}\|Bx-u^{\mathrm{raw}}\|_2^2
  \qquad\text{subject to}\qquad
  Ax=b .
  \label{eq:hdiv_projection_problem}
\end{equation}
Thus the corrected target fluxes stay as close as possible to the raw transfer
in the directed edge-DOF norm while enforcing one exact divergence constraint
per target cell.

The KKT equations are
\begin{equation}
  \begin{bmatrix}
    H & A^T \\
    A & 0
  \end{bmatrix}
  \begin{bmatrix}
    x \\
    \lambda
  \end{bmatrix}
  =
  \begin{bmatrix}
    g \\
    b
  \end{bmatrix},
  \qquad
  H=B^T B,\quad g=B^T u^{\mathrm{raw}} .
  \label{eq:hdiv_projection_kkt}
\end{equation}
Because every unique target edge is seen by one boundary cell or by two
adjacent cells, \(H\) is diagonal.  The implementation therefore uses the Schur
complement
\begin{equation}
  A H^{-1} A^T \lambda = A H^{-1} g - b,
  \qquad
  x = H^{-1}(g - A^T \lambda).
  \label{eq:hdiv_projection_schur}
\end{equation}
On closed surfaces, \(AH^{-1}A^T\) has the expected one-dimensional nullspace
associated with the globally redundant divergence constraint; the implementation
solves Eq.~\eqref{eq:hdiv_projection_schur} with a rank-revealing
pseudoinverse-style dense solve.  The output \(u^{\mathrm{conf}}=Bx\) is a
globally conforming target-edge flux field in the discrete lowest-order
\(H(\mathrm{div})\) sense: every geometric target edge carries a single flux,
and every target cell satisfies its divergence constraint exactly.

\subsection{Higher-Order Polygonal \(H(\mathrm{div})\)-Style Split Moments}

To study higher-order convergence on planar and spherical polygonal patches,
the implementation adds a unified local split-moment family.  The input data on
one source edge \(e\) are Legendre moments of the normal trace,
\begin{equation}
  \mu_{e,m}
  =
  \int_e (\mathbf{u}\cdot \mathbf{n}_e) L_m(s)\, ds,
  \qquad m=0,\dots,p,
  \label{eq:edge-moment-dofs}
\end{equation}
where \(L_m\) is the degree-\(m\) Legendre polynomial on the edge reference
coordinate \(s\in[-1,1]\).  Optional cell vector moments are also admitted,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{c}_{ab}
  =
  \int_K \mathbf{u}\, x^a y^b \, dA,
  \qquad a+b \le r.
  \label{eq:cell-vector-moments}
\end{equation}
In the present study the cell moment order is chosen as \(r=\max(1,p-1)\) for
the higher-order regressions.

The reconstructed field is represented on the centroid-relative source chart as
\begin{equation}
  \uh^{(p)}(\x)
  =
  \sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{div}}} \alpha_i \boldsymbol{\phi}^{\mathrm{div}}_i(\x)
  +
  \sum_{j=1}^{N_{\mathrm{harm}}} \beta_j \boldsymbol{\phi}^{\mathrm{harm}}_j(\x)
  +
  \sum_{k=1}^{N_{\mathrm{bub}}} \gamma_k \boldsymbol{\phi}^{\mathrm{bub}}_k(\x),
  \label{eq:split-moment-field}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{itemize}
  \item \(\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\mathrm{div}}_i\) span a polynomial-divergence
        particular subspace,
  \item \(\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\mathrm{harm}}_j\) are gradients of harmonic
        polynomials, and
  \item \(\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\mathrm{bub}}_k\) span the remaining
        divergence-free completion subspace.
\end{itemize}
For degree \(p\), the dimensions are
\[
  N_{\mathrm{div}}=\frac{p(p+1)}{2},\qquad
  N_{\mathrm{harm}}=2(p+1),\qquad
  N_{\mathrm{bub}}=\frac{p(p+1)}{2},
\]
so the total dimension
\(
N_{\mathrm{div}}+N_{\mathrm{harm}}+N_{\mathrm{bub}}=(p+1)(p+2)
\)
matches \([\mathbb{P}_p(K)]^2\).  The split basis is assembled algebraically in
raw vector-polynomial coordinates, then evaluated in scaled local coordinates
\begin{equation}
  \tilde{\x} = \frac{\x}{h_K},
  \qquad
  h_K = \max\left(\sqrt{|K|}, \max_i \|\x_i\|\right),
  \label{eq:scaled-local-coordinates}
\end{equation}
where \(\x_i\) are the centroid-relative polygon vertices.  In spherical mode,
the same split basis is assembled in each source-cell gnomonic chart, and the
edge moments use the intrinsic great-circle arc coordinate on the physical
edge.

For smooth manufactured fields on nonmatching patches, the higher-order moments
are generally not represented exactly by one finite-dimensional local polynomial
on every polygon.  The implemented solve therefore separates the moment rows
into:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item hard rows \(A_0 \alpha = m_0\), consisting of the zeroth edge moments
        \(\mu_{e,0}\) on every source edge; and
  \item soft rows \(A_+ \alpha \approx m_+\), consisting of the higher edge
        moments \(\mu_{e,m}\) for \(m\ge 1\) and the optional cell vector
        moments.
\end{enumerate}
The local coefficients are then defined by the constrained split-moment
least-squares problem
\begin{equation}
  \min_{\alpha}
  \frac{1}{2}\|W(A_+\alpha - m_+)\|_2^2
  +
  \frac{\lambda}{2}\alpha^T G \alpha
  \qquad \text{subject to} \qquad
  A_0 \alpha = m_0 ,
  \label{eq:projected-moment-problem}
\end{equation}
where \(W\) is a diagonal weight matrix for the soft rows, \(G\) is the local
vector-polynomial mass matrix, and \(\lambda\) is a small regularization
parameter.  Equation~\eqref{eq:projected-moment-problem} is solved by a local
KKT system whenever hard rows are present.  If all rows are treated as exact,
the code reverts to the direct QR or minimum-energy solve used for the exact
polynomial patch test.

This split is the key to the current high-order behavior.  Exact hard
enforcement of \(m_0\) preserves the cellwise conservative flux balance used by
the low-order method, while the soft rows drive asymptotic accuracy for smooth
fields through higher edge and cell moments.  The method is therefore best
described as a \emph{polygonal \(H(\mathrm{div})\)-style split-moment
family}: the reconstructed vector polynomials lie in \([\mathbb{P}_p]^2\subset
H(\mathrm{div})\) locally, the primary edge-flux functional is preserved
exactly, and the divergence-controlled and divergence-free parts of the local
space are explicitly separated.

\paragraph{Metric-corrected orthogonalization.}
In the spherical gnomonic backend, the local vector-polynomial mass matrix~\(G\)
in~\eqref{eq:projected-moment-problem} is assembled with the Hodge/Piola metric
\(J^T\!J / |J|\) from~\eqref{eq:hodge-piola-metric}.  When this
metric-weighted~\(G\) is available, the split basis
in~\eqref{eq:split-moment-field} is orthonormalized against~\(G\) rather than
against the Euclidean identity.  Concretely, each block of mode vectors~\(M\) is
replaced by its metric-orthonormal counterpart~\(Q\) satisfying \(Q^T G\, Q =
I\) via the eigendecomposition of the block Gram \(M^T G\, M = V\, D\, V^T\) and
the substitution \(Q = M\, V D^{-1/2}\).  Cross-block orthogonality is enforced
sequentially: harmonic modes are projected against divergence modes in the
\(G\)-inner product before orthonormalization, and bubble modes are projected
against both divergence and harmonic blocks.  This ensures that the KKT
solver in~\eqref{eq:projected-moment-problem} operates with the correct surface
inner product.

\paragraph{Degree-elevated basis for spherical \(p\ge3\).}
On the sphere, the exact surface vector field pulled back to gnomonic chart
coordinates via the contravariant Piola mapping is a rational function of
\((\xi_1,\xi_2)\) because the Piola involves \(J/|J|\) where
\(|J|=(1+|\boldsymbol{\xi}|^2)^{-3/2}\).  A degree-\(p\) polynomial basis can
approximate this rational field only to polynomial accuracy, and the dominant
approximation error is \(O(h_K^2)\) pointwise, giving an \(O(h_K^3)\) edge-flux
error.  For \(p\le 2\) this is comparable to or smaller than the \(O(h_K^{p+1})\)
asymptotic term, but for \(p\ge 3\) it dominates.

The remedy is \emph{degree elevation}: for \(p\ge3\) in spherical mode, the
vector polynomial degree is raised from~\(p\) to~\(p+2\).  The enlarged basis
\([\mathbb{P}_{p+2}]^2\) spans all products
\(|\boldsymbol{\xi}|^2 \boldsymbol{v}_j(\boldsymbol{\xi})\) for
\(\boldsymbol{v}_j\in[\mathbb{P}_p]^2\), which captures the leading-order
rational correction from the Piola mapping.  The edge and cell moment constraints
remain at order~\(p\) (from the source data), making the system under-determined.
The extra degrees of freedom are resolved by the minimum-energy regularization
in~\eqref{eq:projected-moment-problem}, which selects the smoothest
chart-coordinate field consistent with the physical moment data.

\section{Why Wachspress Coordinates Are Not a Drop-In Replacement}

One of the original alternatives considered for polygonal patches was to use a
Wachspress basis.  This is natural: Wachspress coordinates are rational
generalized barycentric coordinates on convex polygons, reproduce affine
functions, form a partition of unity, and have been used successfully in
conforming polygonal finite elements \cite{Wachspress1975,SukumarTabarraei2004,
GilletteRandBajaj2012}.  The difficulty is not that Wachspress coordinates are
inaccurate.  The difficulty is that the native scalar basis has the wrong
degrees of freedom and the wrong differential structure for this edge-flux
transfer problem.

\subsection{Compatible Flux Transfer Requires an RT-like Structure}

The lowest-order RT analogy has two separate ingredients:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item the cell divergence is constant, \(\nabla\cdot\uh=d_K\), and
  \item the normal trace on each cell edge is represented by one edge flux
        degree of freedom.
\end{enumerate}
On triangles these properties are built into RT0 \cite{RaviartThomas1977,
BoffiBrezziFortin2013}; the RT0 normal component is constant on each edge.
The harmonic mimetic reconstruction uses a slightly different polygonal
realization: the \(\frac{d}{2}\x\) term supplies constant divergence, while
the harmonic gradient part is divergence-free and is fitted to the integrated
edge fluxes through the KKT constraints.  The pointwise edge-normal trace may
vary when higher harmonic modes are present, but the edge flux degree of
freedom is enforced exactly.  Thus, for every clipped subregion \(O\subset K\),
\begin{equation}
  \int_{\partial O}\uh\cdot\n\,ds
  =
  d_K |O|.
  \label{eq:rt_like_overlap}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:rt_like_overlap} is the invariant used by the conservative
overlap and target-cell balance tests.

\begin{proposition}[Obstruction for componentwise Wachspress flux transfer]
Let \(K\) be a convex polygon with at least one non-affine Wachspress coordinate
\(\lambda_i\).  A componentwise vector interpolant
\[
  \mathbf{v}_W(\x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{c}_i\lambda_i(\x),
  \qquad \mathbf{c}_i\in\R^2,
\]
does not define an RT0-like edge-flux space: its divergence is generically
nonconstant in \(K\), and its normal trace is generically nonconstant along an
edge.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The divergence is
\[
  \nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}_W
  =
  \sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{c}_i\cdot\nabla\lambda_i .
\]
If \(\mathbf{v}_W\) had constant divergence for every choice of vector
coefficients, then each directional derivative of each coordinate function
would have to lie in the constants.  That would make every \(\lambda_i\)
affine.  Wachspress coordinates are affine only in the simplex case and in
special degenerate affine cases; for general \(n>3\) polygons they are rational
and non-affine \cite{Wachspress1975,FloaterHormannKos2006}.  Choosing all
coefficients zero except, for example, \(\mathbf{c}_i=(1,0)^T\) gives
\(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}_W=\partial_x\lambda_i\), which is nonconstant whenever
\(\lambda_i\) is non-affine.

On an edge \(e=[x_j,x_{j+1}]\), the only nonzero scalar Wachspress coordinates
are \(\lambda_j\) and \(\lambda_{j+1}\), and they vary linearly along the edge.
The normal trace is therefore
\[
  \mathbf{v}_W\cdot\n_e
  =
  (\mathbf{c}_j\cdot\n_e)\lambda_j
  +
  (\mathbf{c}_{j+1}\cdot\n_e)\lambda_{j+1},
\]
which is constant only if the two endpoint normal components are equal.  A
single integrated edge flux does not enforce that pointwise equality.  Thus the
componentwise scalar Wachspress interpolant does not have the constant
edge-normal trace associated with lowest-order RT flux degrees of freedom.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The statement above is about the direct componentwise use of scalar Wachspress
coordinates.  It does not rule out vector-valued generalized barycentric
constructions.  In particular, generalized Whitney forms can produce H(div)
spaces on convex polytopes \cite{GilletteRandBajaj2016}.  Such a method should
be studied as a new compatible finite element discretization with its own
normal-trace basis, mass matrix, Piola map, and quadrature rules.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Consequences for This Transfer Problem}

For conservative edge transfer, matching only the total flux on a source edge is
not enough.  A target edge can cut through a source cell and can use only a
subsegment of the source boundary or interior.  The line functional applied to
that clipped segment must be consistent with the same source-cell divergence
used by the surrounding overlap balance.  The harmonic mimetic form gives this
consistency analytically through Eq.~\eqref{eq:line_integral} and
Eq.~\eqref{eq:rt_like_overlap}.  A componentwise Wachspress vector fit would
require additional constraints or quadrature to control the nonconstant
divergence and edge-normal trace.  Even then, the reconstruction would no
longer be determined naturally by edge flux degrees of freedom alone.

The practical conclusion is therefore:
\begin{itemize}
  \item scalar Wachspress coordinates are appropriate for scalar H1 polygonal
        interpolation and displacement-like unknowns;
  \item componentwise Wachspress vector interpolation is not a better
        replacement for the current conservative edge-flux transfer; and
  \item Wachspress-adjacent H(div) spaces, especially generalized Whitney-form
        or mixed virtual element constructions, are credible future work but
        should be evaluated as a separate method.
\end{itemize}

\section{Discrete Algorithms}

\subsection*{Algorithm 1: Source-Cell Reconstruction}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Orient the source polygon counter-clockwise in its local chart.
  \item Construct local edge coordinates and outward normals.  In spherical
        mode, first build the source-cell gnomonic chart and project the
        great-circle vertices into that chart.
  \item Read the directed source edge flux vector \(U_f\).
  \item Compute \(d\) from Eq.~\eqref{eq:divergence}.
  \item Assemble \(V\) using Eq.~\eqref{eq:gram}; in spherical mode, replace
        \(\nabla H_i\cdot\nabla H_j\) with the Hodge-weighted form from
        Eq.~\eqref{eq:piola_hodge}.
  \item Assemble \(r\) using Eq.~\eqref{eq:rhs}.
  \item Solve the constrained local system and retain the coefficients
        \((d,a_1,b_1,a_2,b_2)\).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Algorithm 2: Conservative Evaluation on a Target or Overlap Edge}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Convert target or overlap vertices into the source-cell local frame.
  \item For line integrals use Eq.~\eqref{eq:line_integral}.
  \item For overlap conservation, integrate each directed overlap boundary edge
        with the outward normal implied by counter-clockwise order.
  \item Sum edge contributions and compare to \(d_s |O|\).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Algorithm 3: Voronoi Patch Verification}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Generate source and target bounded Voronoi cells by clipping the unit
        square with perpendicular bisectors.
  \item Retain all nondegenerate convex cells.
  \item Set analytic source edge fluxes from \(\mathbf{u}(x,y)=(x,y)\).
  \item Reconstruct every source cell using Algorithm 1.
  \item Clip each source-target pair using convex polygon clipping.
  \item For every nonempty overlap, check Eq.~\eqref{eq:overlap_conservation}.
  \item For every target cell, check that source overlaps cover the target area
        and that the overlap-summed conservative integral equals \(2|K_t|\).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Algorithm 4: Directed Edge Transfer and Sparse Projection}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Enumerate directed source-cell edge DOFs and directed target-cell edge
        DOFs.
  \item For each source cell, build \(B_{K_s}\) from
        Eq.~\eqref{eq:local_reconstruction_matrix}.
  \item For each directed target edge, clip the target segment against every
        source polygon.
  \item For direct transfer, evaluate Eq.~\eqref{eq:edge_transfer} on each
        clipped segment and sum into the target edge flux.
  \item For matrix assembly, apply the segment functional to \(B_{K_s}\) and add
        triplets to the global sparse matrix \(P\) in Eq.~\eqref{eq:sparse_projection}.
  \item Verify that applying \(P\) to the source edge-flux vector reproduces the
        direct transfer.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Algorithm 5: Spherical Source-Chart Edge Transfer}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item For each source cell, normalize mesh vertices, construct a gnomonic
        frame, and project the great-circle polygon into the source chart.
  \item Pull manufactured source fields into the chart with
        Eq.~\eqref{eq:piola_pullback}; for shared manufactured edges, assign one
        conservative geometric flux and the opposite sign to the reversed
        directed neighbor.
  \item Reconstruct source chart coefficients with Algorithm 1.
  \item For each directed target great-circle edge, project both endpoints into
        each source chart.  Since great circles project to straight lines, clip
        this segment against the projected source polygon.
  \item Apply the direct segment functional for transfer, or apply the same
        functional to the source reconstruction matrix for sparse projection.
  \item Verify global conservation, target-cell KKT re-integration, and
        direct-vs-sparse equality with absolute tolerance \(5\times10^{-13}\).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Algorithm 6: Global H(div)-Conforming Target Projection}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Compute the raw directed target-edge transfer with Algorithm 4 or
        Algorithm 5.
  \item Collapse directed target edges to unique geometric target edges by
        matching endpoint coordinates and assigning one canonical orientation.
  \item Assemble the target divergence right-hand side \(b\) from
        Eq.~\eqref{eq:target_divergence_rhs} using the same source-target overlap
        clipping already used for cell averages.
  \item Form the directed-edge map \(B\), the target cell-edge incidence matrix
        \(A\), and the diagonal matrix \(H=B^TB\).
  \item Solve the constrained least-squares problem in
        Eq.~\eqref{eq:hdiv_projection_problem} via the Schur complement in
        Eq.~\eqref{eq:hdiv_projection_schur}.
  \item Recover the directed conforming target-edge fluxes
        \(u^{\mathrm{conf}}=Bx\) and verify \(\|Ax-b\|_\infty\le 5\times10^{-13}\).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Algorithm 7: High-Order Projected Edge-Moment Transfer}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Choose an edge moment order \(p\) and cell moment order \(r=p-1\).
  \item On each source edge, compute the Legendre moments
        \(\mu_{e,m}\) from Eq.~\eqref{eq:edge-moment-dofs}; optionally compute
        the cell vector moments from Eq.~\eqref{eq:cell-vector-moments}.
  \item Build the scaled vector-polynomial basis
        \(\boldsymbol{\psi}_j(\x/h_K)\) from
        Eq.~\eqref{eq:scaled-local-coordinates}.
  \item Assemble the hard zeroth-moment block \(A_0\), the soft higher-moment
        block \(A_+\), the mass matrix \(G\), and the weights \(W\).
  \item Solve the constrained projected problem
        Eq.~\eqref{eq:projected-moment-problem}; for exact polynomial patch
        tests, solve all rows as exact constraints instead.
  \item For each directed target edge, clip the segment against all source
        polygons and integrate the reconstructed polynomial moments on each
        clipped subsegment.
  \item Verify exact conservation of the zeroth target edge moment and measure
        the higher-moment target-edge error under refinement.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Algorithmic Analysis}

Let \(n_K\) be the number of sides of one polygon and let \(q=4\) be the number
of harmonic coefficients in the current level-2 truncation.

\paragraph{Local reconstruction.}
Geometry extraction and edge moment evaluation are \(O(n_K)\).  Assembly of
the Gram matrix is \(O(q^2 n_K)\), and assembly of the right-hand side is
\(O(q n_K)\).  The dense solve is \(O(q^3)\).  Since \(q=4\) is fixed, the
reconstruction cost is effectively linear in the number of polygon sides:
\[
  T_{\mathrm{recon}}(K)=O(n_K).
\]

\paragraph{Overlap evaluation.}
For convex source and target polygons with \(n_s\) and \(n_t\) sides, repeated
half-plane clipping costs \(O(n_s n_t)\).  Once an overlap polygon \(O\) with
\(n_O\) sides is known, the boundary-flux check is \(O(n_O)\).  The reference
algorithm uses deterministic all-pairs candidate enumeration for clarity.  A
production-scale implementation should replace that enumeration with a spatial
index or advancing-front traversal without changing the local clipped-segment
functional.

\paragraph{Edge transfer and projection assembly.}
Let \(E_t\) be the number of directed target edges and \(N_s\) the number of
source cells.  The reference all-pairs algorithm clips each target edge
against each source polygon, so its cost is \(O(E_t N_s \bar n_s)\) for average
source side count \(\bar n_s\).  Sparse matrix assembly has the same geometric
cost plus local coefficient-row multiplication.  The number of nonzeros is
proportional to the number of source-edge basis functions that influence each
clipped target-edge segment.

\paragraph{Memory.}
Each source cell stores \(O(q)\) reconstruction coefficients and each directed
edge stores one flux value.  The local dense matrices are \(q\times q\), and the
sparse projection stores one nonzero block contribution for each source-cell
segment that intersects a directed target edge.

\paragraph{Global target-edge projection.}
Let \(N_g\) be the number of unique geometric target edges and \(N_t\) the
number of target cells.  Collapsing directed target edges to unique edges is
\(O(E_t)\) once endpoint keys are available.  Assembly of \(A\), \(B\), and the
right-hand side \(b\) is linear in the number of directed target edges plus the
existing overlap-clipping cost.  In the present serial prototype,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:hdiv_projection_schur} is solved with a dense rank-revealing
factorization on the \(N_t\times N_t\) Schur complement, so the additional solve
cost is \(O(N_t^3)\) and the storage cost is \(O(N_t^2)\).  This is acceptable
for the present verification problems but should be replaced by a sparse graph
solver for large target meshes.

\paragraph{High-order split-moment family.}
For one polygon, let \(p\) be the edge moment order and let
\(B_p=(p+1)(p+2)\) be the total number of split-basis functions in
\([\mathbb{P}_p]^2\).  Let \(n_K\) be the number of polygon edges and
\(M_p=n_K(p+1)\) the number of edge moments.  With cell moment order
\(r=\max(1,p-1)\), the number of vector cell moments is \((r+1)(r+2)\).  Local
assembly therefore has four dominant pieces: formation of the raw dense mass
matrix \(G\) with cost \(O(B_p^2 n_q n_K)\), assembly of the split-basis map
from polynomial-divergence, harmonic-gradient, and divergence-free completion
modes, formation of the edge and cell moment matrices with cost
\(O(B_p(M_p + (r+1)(r+2)) n_q)\), and a dense KKT or QR solve with cost
\(O(B_p^3)\).  For the present study, \(p\in\{1,2,3\}\), so these costs remain
small enough that geometry and clipping still dominate the total runtime.  The
important practical point is conditioning rather than asymptotic cost: without
the scaled coordinates in Eq.~\eqref{eq:scaled-local-coordinates}, the
\(p=3\) refined-patch systems develop visibly worse error plateaus on irregular
polygons.

\subsection{Constant-Field Patch Test}

The patch test uses a unit-square source cell and an interior nonmatching target
quadrilateral.  With analytic field \(\mathbf{u}=(1,1)\), the reconstruction
recovers
\[
  d=0,\qquad a_1=1,\qquad b_1=1,\qquad a_2=b_2=0
\]
to roundoff.  The interior line integral from
\((-0.2,-0.3)\) to \((0.4,0.2)\) in source-local coordinates is exactly \(1.1\).
The target edge fluxes are \((-0.55,0.55,0.55,-0.55)\), and their sum is zero.
The maximum printed absolute error in the coefficient, line-integral, and target
edge checks is zero at the displayed precision.

\subsection{Rectangular Nonmatching Mesh Test}

The rectangular test uses a \(2\times2\) source grid and a nonmatching \(3\times3\)
target grid over the same unit square.  The analytic source field is
\(\mathbf{u}=(x,y)\), so \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}=2\).  The test checks every one
of the 16 source-target rectangular intersections.  Each overlap satisfies
Eq.~\eqref{eq:overlap_conservation} to roundoff.  The aggregate overlap area is
1 and the aggregate divergence integral is 2.
The largest printed per-overlap boundary-flux error is
\(2.78\times10^{-17}\).  The aggregate source-target area error is
\(2.22\times10^{-16}\), and the aggregate integral error relative to
\(\int_\Omega\nabla\cdot(x,y)\,dA=2\) is \(4.44\times10^{-16}\).
The edge-wise transfer then computes 36 directed target edge fluxes.  Each
target edge agrees with the analytic integral of \(\mathbf{u}(x,y)=(x,y)\) to
roundoff, and each target cell satisfies
\[
  \sum_{e_t\subset\partial K_t} U^t_{e_t}=2|K_t|.
\]
The assembled sparse projection for this case has 36 rows, 16 columns, and 80
nonzeros.  Applying this matrix to the directed source edge flux vector
reproduces the direct edge-transfer result to roundoff.

\subsection{Voronoi n-gon Patch Test}
The Voronoi test uses two different bounded Voronoi meshes over the unit square.
The source mesh side-count histogram is
\[
  n=4:2,\qquad n=5:6,\qquad n=6:1,
\]
so seven source cells have more than four sides.  The target mesh side-count
histogram is
\[
  n=4:4,\qquad n=5:5,\qquad n=6:5,
\]
so ten target cells have more than four sides.  The source-target clipping
produces 36 nonempty overlaps, including 23 n-gon to n-gon overlaps where both
the source and target cells have more than four sides.

Every overlap satisfies Eq.~\eqref{eq:overlap_conservation}.  Each target cell
is covered by source overlaps to roundoff.  The aggregate checks are:
\[
  \sum |K_s\cap K_t| = 1,\qquad
  \sum \int_{\partial(K_s\cap K_t)}{\uh}_s\cdot\n\,ds = 2,\qquad
  \int_\Omega \nabla\cdot(x,y)\,dA = 2.
\]
The largest printed overlap or aggregate residual is \(4.44\times10^{-16}\),
well below the \(5\times10^{-13}\) conservation tolerance used throughout the
validation study.
The edge-wise Voronoi transfer computes 71 directed target edge fluxes.  Every
target edge agrees with the analytic global linear field integral to roundoff,
and every target cell satisfies the same edge-flux divergence check
\(\sum U^t_{e_t}=2|K_t|\).

\subsection{Edge Error and Cell-Divergence Visualizations}

The quantitative convergence diagnostics are edge-based: for each directed
target edge \(e\), the transferred flux is compared with the manufactured
integral \(\int_e \mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{exact}}\cdot\n\,ds\).  Figures
\ref{fig:error_quad}--\ref{fig:error_vv} do not plot the vector solution
\(\mathbf{u}\) itself.  They show a complementary scalar cell quantity, the
cell-averaged divergence recovered from the directed edge fluxes,
\[
  \frac{1}{|K|}\sum_{e\subset\partial K} U^t_e .
\]
This distinction matters.  Edge errors assess the transferred H(div) degrees of
freedom, while these plots show how the conservative edge fluxes assemble into
macroscopic cell balances.  In particular, a divergence-free manufactured field
can have exact source and exact target panels that are zero to roundoff, while
the reconstructed target panel is nonzero on coarse or irregular cells because
the transferred edge fluxes do not close exactly on each target cell.  That is
a local flux-balance defect, not a pointwise vector-field value.

Figure~\ref{fig:error_quad} shows a harmonic field transferred from a source
quad mesh to a refined target quad mesh.  Figures~\ref{fig:error_vq} and
\ref{fig:error_vv} show the same diagnostic idea for Voronoi-to-quad and
Voronoi-to-Voronoi configurations.

\subsection{Global H(div)-Conforming Target Projection}

The new global target-skeleton correction was validated on the same planar
Voronoi-to-Voronoi family that exposes the raw target-cell closure defect most
clearly.  Two manufactured fields were used:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item the exact linear field \(\mathbf{u}(x,y)=(x,y)\), for which the source
        reconstruction and target-cell divergence right-hand side are exact; and
  \item the smooth divergence-free field
        \(\mathbf{u}(x,y)=(\sin(\pi x)\cos(\pi y),-\cos(\pi x)\sin(\pi y))\),
        for which the raw nonmatching transfer is not globally conforming on
        coarse Voronoi meshes.
\end{enumerate}

For the exact linear field, the raw transfer already satisfies the target-cell
divergence constraints to roundoff, and the conforming correction is therefore
negligible.  The maximum raw target-cell residual is
\(2.01\times10^{-16}\), the maximum corrected residual is
\(5.55\times10^{-17}\), and the relative correction norm is
\(4.50\times10^{-16}\).  The assembled target divergence right-hand side agrees
with the exact value \(2|K_t|\) to \(1.18\times10^{-16}\).  The maximum raw
interior-edge jump defect is already only \(1.39\times10^{-16}\), and the
constrained field preserves that single-valued target skeleton.

For the smooth divergence-free field, the raw target-cell closure defect is
substantial on coarse Voronoi meshes but decreases under refinement.  The new
global projection removes that defect to roundoff at every resolution while
changing the directed edge field only modestly.  In fact, the corrected edge
field slightly reduces the directed edge-flux error in this study because the
least-squares correction removes low-order target-cell imbalance.  The
visual comparison in Figure~\ref{fig:error_vv_hdiv} makes the same point
geometrically: the raw local transfer carries a visible cell-balance defect on
the target patch, whereas the constrained skeleton solve collapses that defect
to values indistinguishable from roundoff on the same mesh.  Figure
\ref{fig:error_vv_edge_jump} shows that this is not an edge-continuity issue:
the merged-target raw transfer already has interior-edge jumps at roundoff, and
the global solve preserves that single-valued target-edge skeleton while
correcting the target-cell balance residual.  In the present Voronoi sequence,
the maximum raw interior-edge jump stays between \(8.67\times10^{-17}\) and
\(1.11\times10^{-16}\), while the maximum constrained jump is zero to printed
precision.

\subsection{High-Order Split-Moment Convergence}

The high-order study uses a smooth variable-divergence manufactured field on
the unit square,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{u}(x,y)=
  \begin{bmatrix}
    e^{0.2x}\bigl(\sin(\pi x)+0.2y^2\bigr)+0.05x^3 \\
    e^{0.2y}\bigl(\cos(\pi y)+0.2xy\bigr)+0.05y^3
  \end{bmatrix}.
  \label{eq:high-order-test-field}
\end{equation}
This field was chosen so that the convergence study measures asymptotic
behavior on a non-polynomial target rather than exact recovery of a field that
already lies in the local polynomial space.  Two nonmatching refinement
families were used:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item quad-to-quad meshes with \((4\to5)\), \((8\to9)\), and \((16\to17)\)
        source-target resolutions; and
  \item Voronoi-to-Voronoi meshes with
        \((49\to64)\), \((100\to121)\), and \((196\to225)\) deterministic
        Halton seed counts.
\end{enumerate}

The \(p=1\) line in this study uses the harmonic-compatible lowest-order remap,
while the \(p=2,3\) lines use the split-moment enrichment from
Eq.~\eqref{eq:split-moment-field} and the constrained fit in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:projected-moment-problem}.  In all cases, the zeroth edge moment
is imposed exactly, so the global conservation residual remains at roundoff
throughout the refinement sequence.

Figure~\ref{fig:high-order-hdiv-convergence} shows the resulting relative
directed target-edge flux error curves.  On quad-to-quad meshes, the observed
average rates are \(2.26\), \(3.39\), and \(4.46\) for \(p=1,2,3\).  On the
Voronoi-to-Voronoi sequence, the observed average rates are \(1.89\), \(2.77\),
and \(3.34\) for \(p=1,2,3\).  The important point is that the harmonic
baseline and split-moment enrichments show systematic order separation:
enriching the edge moments and the split local space produces visibly steeper
error decay on both structured and unstructured polygon patches.

\subsection{Spherical Structured and Unstructured Tests}

The spherical geometry unit test verifies the local chart primitives before any
mesh transfer is attempted.  It checks a known octant-triangle area
\(\pi/2\), an independent spherical-excess quadrilateral area, a
gnomonic project/inverse round trip, the fact that a great-circle midpoint lies
on the projected straight segment, and source-cell KKT re-integration of all
input edge fluxes to \(5\times10^{-13}\).

The structured spherical transfer test uses cubed-sphere meshes and the smooth
surface field
\[
  f(x,y,z)=\frac{1}{2}(3z^2-1),\qquad
  \mathbf{u}=\nabla_S f.
\]
The no-argument regression test runs four cases: \(4\to4\) identity,
\(4\to6\) refinement, \(6\to4\) coarsening, and \(6\to8\) refinement.  The
largest global conservation residual among these cases is
\(4.36\times10^{-15}\), the largest direct-vs-sparse residual is
\(2.78\times10^{-16}\), and the largest target-cell KKT edge re-integration
residual is \(2.24\times10^{-16}\).  The identity case transfers all directed
edge DOFs with maximum error \(2.78\times10^{-16}\).  With the full
Hodge/Piola-consistent spherical Gram matrix, the relative edge-flux error is
\(6.51\times10^{-2}\) on \(4\to6\), \(3.37\times10^{-2}\) on \(6\to4\), and
\(3.05\times10^{-2}\) on \(6\to8\), while preserving the same machine-precision
conservation checks.

The same structured cases were also passed through the new global target-edge
\(H(\mathrm{div})\) projection.  The maximum corrected target-cell divergence
residual is \(2.22\times10^{-16}\) on the identity case,
\(1.25\times10^{-16}\) on \(4\to6\), \(1.11\times10^{-16}\) on \(6\to4\), and
\(8.33\times10^{-17}\) on \(6\to8\).  The relative correction norm decreases
from \(1.28\times10^{-2}\) on \(4\to6\) to \(5.75\times10^{-3}\) on \(6\to8\),
so the spherical raw transfer also approaches the globally conforming target
skeleton under refinement.

To quantify refinement beyond the default acceptance cases, an additional sweep
was run on \((3\to5), (4\to6), (6\to8), (8\to10), (12\to14), (16\to18)\)
structured cubed-sphere transfers.  The measured relative edge-flux errors were
\(1.03\times10^{-1}\), \(6.51\times10^{-2}\), \(3.05\times10^{-2}\),
\(1.76\times10^{-2}\), \(7.98\times10^{-3}\), and \(4.53\times10^{-3}\), with
observed edge-error convergence rates \(1.61\), \(1.87\), \(1.91\), \(1.95\),
and \(1.97\) between successive levels.  The direct target cell-average
\(L^1\) error drops from \(1.00\) on \(3\to5\) to \(4.74\times10^{-2}\) on
\(16\to18\), while the reconstructed target cell-average \(L^1\) error drops
from \(1.40\) to \(8.87\times10^{-2}\).  Thus the spherical extension is not
merely conservative; on these smooth cubed-sphere tests it remains strongly
convergent under refinement.

The same cubed-sphere source-chart machinery was then exercised with the
split-moment family using clean factor-of-2 refinement with non-commensurate
source and target resolutions to avoid aliasing:
\((4\to7), (8\to14), (16\to28)\).  For the zeroth edge moment, the
average convergence rates are \(1.75\), \(3.08\), and \(4.57\) for
\(p=1,2,3\).  When all transferred edge moments are included in the error norm,
the corresponding average rates are \(1.75\), \(3.07\), and \(4.09\).  The
finest structured relative zeroth-moment errors are
\(5.22\times10^{-3}\), \(1.50\times10^{-4}\), and \(1.22\times10^{-5}\) for
\(p=1,2,3\), and every structured case still reduces the conforming target-cell
divergence residual to \(O(10^{-16})\).

The cubed-sphere \(p=3\) rate of \(4.57\) is a direct consequence of the
degree-elevated basis described in Section~3.9: without the elevation, the
\(O(h^3)\) Piola-rational approximation error limited the rate to \(\sim1.8\).
The fine-pair rate is \(5.52\), indicating that the degree-elevated basis
captures the Piola correction well enough for the actual convergence to
approach \(O(h^5)\) at these resolutions.  This verifies that the higher-order
split basis, the intrinsic great-circle edge moments, the metric-corrected
orthogonalization, and the gnomonic/Piola transfer remain compatible on the
sphere.

The spherical unstructured test builds deterministic n-sided Voronoi patches in

The higher-order spherical study was then extended to deterministic Voronoi

harmonic \(p=1\) baseline, while the conforming target-edge projection keeps
the target-cell divergence residual at roundoff.

\subsection{Discussion}

The results show that the conservative property is controlled by three local
ingredients: exact reconstruction of the polynomial moments used by the
level-2 basis, exact reduction over each clipped overlap boundary, and
edge-wise target-segment clipping with the target edge normal.  For the
manufactured field \(\mathbf{u}=(x,y)\), the reconstructed divergence is
constant and equal to 2 on every source cell.  Therefore the expected overlap
flux is \(2|O|\), and the target-cell edge flux sum is \(2|K_t|\).  The
residuals in Table~\ref{tab:verification-summary} measure only floating-point
and clipping-roundoff effects.

The planar Voronoi test rules out a common false positive in polygon remapping:
passing only quadrilateral or axis-aligned cases.  Here both source
and target meshes contain five- and six-sided cells, and 23 nonempty
intersections involve n-gons on both sides.  The exact overlap identity holds on
each of those intersections, and the directed-edge checks verify that the
remapped edge data themselves, not only overlap-cell diagnostics, reproduce the
analytic target edge integrals.

The new global target-edge correction resolves the specific limitation exposed
by Fig.~\ref{fig:error_vv}.  Equation~\eqref{eq:target_balance_with_jumps}
shows that the raw nonmatching target-cell edge sum is contaminated by source
interface jumps unless adjacent source-cell reconstructions induce the same
normal trace on their shared edge.  Table~\ref{tab:hdiv-projection} confirms
the expected behavior.  For the exact linear field, the correction is
effectively zero because the raw transfer is already globally compatible.  For
the smooth divergence-free Voronoi sequence, the raw target-cell closure defect
decreases under refinement, and the conforming correction drives it to roundoff
without destroying the directed edge accuracy.  This is the right interpretation
of convergence toward a globally conforming target skeleton: the local harmonic
source reconstruction stays unchanged, while the target-edge field approaches
the globally constrained solution as the mesh is refined.

The high-order planar study adds a second, complementary message.  Exact hard
conservation of the zeroth edge moment is sufficient to keep the transfer
conservative at every order, but it is not sufficient by itself to obtain
higher-order convergence for smooth variable-divergence fields.  The observed
order increase in Figure~\ref{fig:high-order-hdiv-convergence} comes from the
combination of enriched edge moments, optional cell vector moments, and the
constrained split-moment fit in Eq.~\eqref{eq:projected-moment-problem}.  The
quad rates of \(2.26\), \(3.39\), and \(4.46\) are close to the expected
\(p+1\)-type trend for a smooth field on a structured refinement sequence.
The Voronoi rates are lower but still clearly order-separated, which is the
relevant practical result on irregular polygonal patches.  This is also the
reason the current report describes the method as a split-moment polygonal
\(H(\mathrm{div})\)-style family rather than as a finished compatible polygonal
finite element space: the conservation mechanism is exact, while the
higher-moment accuracy mechanism is presently realized through a local
constrained least-squares fit rather than a fully commuting global
discretization.

The spherical results show that the same idea extends to curved manifolds when
the geometry is handled in a source-cell chart and the Piola map is used for
fluxes.  The cubed-sphere \(N\to N\) identity case is particularly important:
it would fail if target edges coincident with source edges were double counted
by adjacent source cells.  The unstructured spherical Voronoi test is the
strongest current spherical result because it verifies conservation and sparse
operator equivalence on n-sided great-circle polygons rather than on only
quad-like cells.

The additional convergence sweep clarifies the role of the spherical inner
product.  The Hodge/Piola correction is methodologically necessary because the
spherical least-squares norm must be a surface vector norm, not a scalar
area-weighted chart norm.  Numerically, however, it changes the error constants
only modestly.  The dominant observation remains the same: once the edge-flux
constraints, source-chart clipping, and direct-vs-sparse consistency are all
enforced, the smooth cubed-sphere regression sequence converges at a rate close
to second order in the directed target-edge norms.  The corrected metric
therefore strengthens the formulation without changing the basic convergence
interpretation.

A scalar control experiment was also added.  It performs a piecewise-constant
conservative remap of cell averages for the same zonal spherical harmonic using
exact spherical overlap areas recovered from the inverse gnomonic map.  The
test now verifies both complete target coverage and global scalar conservation
to the same \(5\times10^{-13}\) tolerance used by the vector transfer.  Its
convergence remains first order, which is the expected behavior for a
piecewise-constant overlap remap.  That makes it a useful geometry-only control:
it verifies the spherical overlap and clipping machinery independently of the
higher-order vector reconstruction, but it is not intended as a sharp accuracy
upper bound for the edge-based method.

The visualizations also clarify what remains outside this phase.  The clipping
kernel assumes convex cells that fit in the relevant source gnomonic hemisphere;
it does not cover nonconvex polygons or target edges crossing a projection
singularity.  Those extensions should preserve the same acceptance criteria:
global conservation, target-cell KKT re-integration, and direct-vs-sparse
agreement at \(5\times10^{-13}\).

\section{Assumptions and Limitations}

\begin{itemize}
  \item Spherical v1 assumes unit-sphere, convex, great-circle cells that fit in
        the source-cell gnomonic hemisphere.
  \item The harmonic mimetic reconstruction used for the planar/spherical base
        method is level-2 only.  This remains the practical stable choice
        emphasized by Perot and Chartrand for polygon meshes
        \cite{PerotChartrand2021}.  The new higher-order family uses a split
        polynomial-divergence / harmonic-gradient / divergence-free completion
        hierarchy rather than a direct higher-level harmonic truncation.
  \item Candidate search is deterministic all-pairs.  A production
        source-target transfer should pair the same local kernels with spatial
        search or an advancing-front traversal.
  \item Tests use manufactured analytic fields and deterministic serial meshes.
        They verify local conservation, sparse operator equivalence, and
        refinement behavior, not global PDE accuracy.
  \item The scalar control test is intentionally piecewise constant: it uses
        exact spherical overlap areas and source cell averages to isolate
        geometry and clipping.  Its first-order behavior therefore should not be
        interpreted as an upper bound on the edge-flux remap accuracy.
  \item The sparse projection currently uses directed cell-edge DOFs.  Shared
        mesh-edge collapse requires explicit orientation metadata.
  \item The new global correction is a conforming \emph{target-edge skeleton}
        projection.  It does not yet construct a single globally conforming
        target-cell interior vector field; target-cell reconstruction still
        proceeds cell by cell from the corrected edge data.
  \item The unified split-moment family now covers \(p=1,2,3\) in planar
        charts, structured spherical gnomonic charts, and deterministic
        spherical Voronoi chart patches.  Broader spherical studies on mixed
        topologies and larger realistic meshes remain future work.
  \item MPI, distributed ownership, ghosting, and parallel I/O are intentionally
        out of scope for this phase.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusions and Future Research Directions}

The present study demonstrates that the level-2 harmonic-polynomial mimetic
reconstruction can be verified on nonmatching planar and spherical polygon
patches.  The planar tests validate
the original overlap and directed-edge transfer invariants.  The spherical tests
validate the gnomonic-chart extension, the contravariant Piola flux mapping,
structured cubed-sphere identity/refinement/coarsening, unstructured n-sided
spherical Voronoi patches, and sparse directed edge projection.  The strongest
current result is that both direct edge transfer and the assembled sparse matrix
produce the same directed target edge DOFs to the \(5\times10^{-13}\)
conservation standard on planar, spherical structured, and spherical
unstructured cases, while the smooth cubed-sphere regression sequence shows
near-second-order decay of the relative edge-flux error.

The unified higher-order split-moment family adds a second verified result.  By
enriching the source data with edge moments and fitting a local field in a
split polynomial-divergence / harmonic-gradient / divergence-free completion
space while keeping the zeroth edge moment exact as a hard constraint, the
method achieves clear order separation on nonmatching polygon patches.  The
quad-to-quad study yields average relative edge-flux rates of \(2.26\),
\(3.39\), and \(4.46\) for \(p=1,2,3\).  On the current Voronoi-to-Voronoi
sequence, the corresponding rates are \(1.89\), \(2.77\), and \(3.34\).  These
results show that higher-order accuracy can be added without sacrificing
machine-precision conservation of the primary edge-flux DOF.  The same
hierarchy now also has verified spherical convergence studies using clean
factor-of-2 refinement with non-commensurate source and target meshes: on the
cubed-sphere sequence the zeroth-moment rates are \(1.75\), \(3.08\), and
\(4.57\), while on the deterministic spherical Voronoi-patch sequence they are
\(2.05\), \(2.52\), and \(3.62\).  The cubed-sphere \(p=3\) rate of \(4.57\) is
achieved via degree elevation of the polynomial basis by two orders, which
captures the leading rational correction from the contravariant Piola mapping.

The added spherical diagnostics sharpen the interpretation of the method.
Switching the cell-centered \(L^1\) norm to physical spherical area is a
necessary diagnostic correction.  Metric-weighting the reconstruction Gram
matrix using the Hodge/Piola tensor ensures the constrained least-squares solve
minimizes the correct surface energy functional.  The metric-corrected basis
orthogonalization ensures the split-basis subspaces are properly separated in
the surface inner product.

The key accuracy advance on the sphere is the degree-elevated basis for
\(p\ge3\).  The exact surface field in gnomonic chart coordinates is rational
(the Piola mapping involves \(J/|J|\) with \(|J|=(1+|\boldsymbol{\xi}|^2)^{-3/2}\)),
and a degree-\(p\) polynomial can approximate this rational field only to
\(O(h_K^3)\) edge-flux accuracy.  Elevating the polynomial degree from~\(p\)
to~\(p+2\) adds the \(|\boldsymbol{\xi}|^2\) correction modes needed to
capture the leading rational term, breaking the \(O(h^3)\) floor and recovering
the expected \(O(h^{p+1})\) asymptotic convergence.  The cubed-sphere \(p=3\)
average rate improves from~1.77 (pre-elevation) to~4.57 (with elevation),
with the fine-pair rate reaching~5.52.

The Wachspress comparison clarifies the method selection.  Scalar Wachspress
coordinates remain valuable for conforming H1 polygonal discretizations, but a
componentwise vertex-based Wachspress vector interpolant does not reproduce the
constant cell divergence or native edge-flux degree-of-freedom structure needed
by this transfer.  For this edge-wise conservative transfer, the harmonic
mimetic basis is therefore the better primary method.  Wachspress-based
compatible vector constructions should be kept as future work only when
formulated as genuine H(div) spaces, such as generalized Whitney forms or mixed
virtual elements.

The added global target-edge projection changes the practical scope of the
method.  The local harmonic reconstruction remains the source-side engine for
exact edge-flux recovery and clipped-edge evaluation.  The new constrained
target solve then converts the raw directed target-edge field into a discrete
globally conforming \(H(\mathrm{div})\) flux field with exact target-cell
divergence constraints.  For divergence-free source reconstructions this means
that every target cell is also divergence-free to machine precision after the
projection, even on coarse nonmatching Voronoi meshes where the raw transfer can
show visible closure defects.

Taken together, the current repository now contains three numerically distinct
but compatible layers: a verified low-order harmonic mimetic remap, a global
target-edge conforming correction, and a unified higher-order split-moment
extension that covers \(p=1,2,3\) on planar polygons, structured spherical
charts, and deterministic spherical Voronoi patches.  The remaining gap to a
full polygonal compatible finite element framework is therefore no longer the
absence of conservative high-order evidence; it is the absence of a single
globally conforming cell-interior target space and broader verification on
mixed-topology and production-scale spherical meshes.

\subsection*{Future Research Directions}
Building on the verified serial planar and spherical framework, the following
logical paths are proposed for future research:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Compatible barycentric H(div) spaces}: Study generalized
    Whitney-form bases built from Wachspress, mean-value, or harmonic
    coordinates \cite{GilletteRandBajaj2016}.  The acceptance criteria should
    be the same as in this manuscript: exact source-edge flux recovery,
    target-cell balance at \(5\times10^{-13}\), and sparse/direct transfer
    equivalence.  This is the mathematically appropriate way to revisit
    Wachspress ideas.
    \item \textbf{Mixed virtual element transfer}: Evaluate H(div)-conforming
    VEM and mixed VEM spaces on the same source-target transfer tests
    \cite{BrezziFalkMarini2014,BeiraoDaVeiga2016,BeiraoDaVeigaMixed2016}.
    VEM may reduce explicit rational basis evaluation while retaining
    polynomial consistency and flux DOFs.
    \item \textbf{High-order conservative remapping links}: Compare the
    directed edge-flux operator with high-order cell-average conservative
    remapping frameworks such as SCRIP-style remapping and TempestRemap
    \cite{Jones1999,UllrichTaylor2015,UllrichDevendranJohansen2016}.  This
    would clarify when edge-flux transfer and scalar conservative remapping can
    share overlap geometry and sparse operator infrastructure.
    \item \textbf{Higher-order spherical controls}: Replace the current
    piecewise-constant scalar control with a higher-order conservative
    spherical remapping baseline so that geometry-only and vector-space
    effects can be separated more sharply.
    \item \textbf{Spherical Robustness}: Extend beyond convex cells in one
    gnomonic hemisphere, add projection-singularity handling, and validate
    against larger realistic spherical meshes.
    \item \textbf{Broader spherical high-order validation}: Extend the present
    cubed-sphere and deterministic Voronoi-patch regressions to mixed
    Voronoi/cubed-sphere transfers, larger full-sphere meshes, and more
    challenging great-circle topologies, and measure whether the current
    structured-versus-unstructured order separation persists there.
    \item \textbf{Globally conforming target interiors}: Extend the present
    target-edge conforming projection to a single compatible target-cell
    interior field so that higher-order moment transfer and global
    \(H(\mathrm{div})\) conformity are enforced in one space rather than by a
    skeleton correction followed by cellwise reconstruction.
    \item \textbf{3D Polyhedral Support}: Extend the geometric intersection and
    interpolation logic to fully three-dimensional polyhedra.  This would
    require volumetric H(div) reconstruction, surface clipping, and flux
    balance over polyhedral intersection boundaries.
    \item \textbf{Integration}: Package the library into a modular component
    for larger simulation frameworks.  Distributed mesh loading, MPI halo
    exchange, and language bindings should remain downstream of the serial
    numerical invariants.
\end{itemize}

At that point the same overlap invariant, Eq.~\eqref{eq:overlap_conservation},
should remain the primary acceptance test for these generalized topologies.
